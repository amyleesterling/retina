Direction selectivity

A bar of light sweeps across the retina in eight directions in turn. Most cells do not care which way it moves. A minority care a great deal, and they are not a separate structure you could point to in the tissue: they sit interleaved with everything else, and the only thing that distinguishes them is what they do.

prefers rightward prefers upward prefers leftward prefers downward
Colour wheels usually deserve suspicion, because they imply an order that the data does not have. This is the exception. Preferred direction is an angle, so it needs a map that wraps, where the value just below 360 degrees sits next to the value just above zero. The four colours are the CA3 house palette spaced evenly around the circle, so the map still wraps and still means an angle without becoming a rainbow.

How the direction gets computed

Direction selectivity is not made in the brain. It is already finished by the time the signal leaves the eye, and the cell most responsible is the starburst amacrine cell. Its dendrites radiate from the cell body like a firework, and the direction computation happens along those individual branches.

The 24 shown below are a sample, not the population. They are the starburst cells that fall inside the calcium imaged set, and the reconstruction holds several hundred more. That matters for what this film can and cannot be used to claim: the real sheet is far denser than what you are about to watch, and any impression you get here about how much neighbouring starburst cells overlap is an artefact of my sampling rather than a fact about retina. What survives the sampling is the depth, because where a cell puts its dendrites does not depend on which other cells I happened to include.

The turn is a real rotation of the reconstruction, not a second render from a second angle, and the sag you can see across the band is real too. This tissue was not cut perfectly parallel to its own layers. I have not flattened it, because the flattening would be the only fictional thing in the frame.

The bar itself is not drawn. Two attempts put a bright slab across the mosaic and both hid the cells they were meant to explain, so the sweep is carried by the cells lighting in sequence instead, each following its own measured tuning curve. The direction being swept is shown on the compass in the corner.

Joining two experiments

The hard part of this was not rendering. It was deciding which reconstructed cell is which recorded cell, and the obvious routes both failed. Matching on segment identifiers returned nothing, because those identifiers are versioned and get rewritten every time somebody proofreads a merge. Matching on supervoxels did better and still recovered only a small fraction.

What worked was the crudest option: cell body coordinates. A soma is a large object in a fixed place, and both experiments recorded where they are. That match is the reason there is anything on this page.

Everything on this page is drawn from that intersection, which is much smaller than either experiment. The reconstruction contains far more cells than were imaged, and the recordings cover cells that have not been reconstructed. Any count below is a count of the overlap, and should never be read as a count of the dataset.

Ten thousand bipolar cells

Bipolar cells are the retina's relay. They carry the signal from the photoreceptors to the ganglion cells that send it to the brain, and on the way they split it into more than a dozen parallel channels, one for each type. In October 2026 the citizen scientists of EyeWire II finished proofreading their 10,000th bipolar cell in this reconstruction, every one checked branch by branch by hand.

The film and the model below show 981 of them: every proofread bipolar cell whose cell body sits within about 80 micrometres of one point in the volume, roughly a tenth of the total. Nineteen cells nearer that point are left out, because their recorded position or their branches put them well outside the patch. The types are a classifier's predictions, not labels anyone drew by hand.

Unlike the two films above, the bipolar film was not rendered in Blender. It is a web page: the meshes come straight from the segmentation through CAVE, are drawn with three.js, and a browser with no screen captured it one frame at a time. The model above is that page with the controls left on, each cell simplified to about two thousand triangles so the whole patch loads in one go.
